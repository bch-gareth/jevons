# Cash Unbound

**post-conf_v0.2.1 | 2026-11-15**

## Money was never one thing

Ask most people what money is and, if they answer at all, they reach not for a
definition but for a list. Money is a medium of exchange — the thing you hand
over. It is a unit of account — the yardstick prices are quoted in. It is a store
of value — the thing you can hold and hope to spend later. This is one of the
few bits of economics almost everyone has picked up without studying it. And it
is worth noticing what *kind* of answer it is. It does not tell you what money
is. It tells you what money does. The definition fixes money by what it does,
not what it is. That small move, made for convenience, carries the whole
argument of this booklet.

So to be clear if you were wondering, the 'binding' I refer to in the title is
not about the binding of money to something else but the bundling of money's
*functions* into a single object.

A list of jobs is not the same thing as a single object obliged to do all of
them at once. For as long as anyone now living can remember, one object has done
them all — the note in your pocket is medium, store and measure together — and so
the jobs and the object have come to feel like the same fact. I think they
are not, and never permanently were. Money's functions can be separated. They
have come apart before, and they can come apart again.

This is the companion piece to my Cash 3.0 2026 talk, written for anyone who heard
me say I would "skip the detail — pick up the booklet." It has more in it than the
spoken version and is also meant to stand on its own, so no part of the argument is
left resting on something you had to be in the room to hear.

You may already be bracing for a familiar fight. Inside and around crypto there
is a long-running argument about which money is the *real* money — store-of-value
against medium-of-exchange, hard money against emissions, one ticker against another.
This booklet does not pick a side in that argument. It does something stranger
and, I think, more useful: it walks away from it. If money's jobs can be done by
different things, then "which single money wins?" is the wrong question, and much
of the heat drains out of the fixture. That move — stepping outside the match
rather than getting involved in it — is the spine of everything that follows, and
it is why the thesis is oddly hard to hear from inside the arena.

Two guardrails, set down now so they carry through. First, the claim is
*permissive*. It is not that one asset *mustn't* serve every function — it is that
it needn't. If people freely choose to pile ease of transfer, measurement and 
storage onto a single asset because it suits them, that is the thesis working, not
failing. The objection is only ever to *coerced* bundling, never to things that
happen to or work better when they travel together by choice. This is a big-tent 
argument; it takes nothing away from anyone's favourite money.

Second, the engine. For millennia, first the friction of trade and later the
machinery of the state compelled money's functions into one object. Digital cash
removes the compulsion. What was mandatory becomes merely available — and once a
thing is genuinely optional, it is, by definition, no longer a bind. Bundling
survives only as one choice on a shelf beside others.

That points at the payoff, which the later chapters cover and which I will only
preview here: unbundling does not abolish money's costs or hand you a perfect
money. There is no perfect money, and this booklet will not pretend to sell one.
What it does is take the compromises that a single all-purpose money kept hidden
inside one price and lay them out as a legible, selectable menu. The win is a
menu, not a champion coin. Hold that thought; the case for it comes later.

The next chapter is history — how the functions were bound in the first place, 
why the binding was never quite total, and why the present moment is genuinely 
new. The chapter after that is a single analogy, and the fight that analogy 
dissolves.

## How the functions were bound

One note before the history proper. This is a history of what money *does*,
and only from a single perspective — and it aims only at a rough approximation,
enough to make the shape visible. That shape is an arc, and this chapter walks
it end to end.

### The fourth function, folded into the second

We began with the three functions almost everyone reaches for: medium of
exchange, unit of account, store of value. In 1875 W. S. Jevons counted a
fourth — a standard of *deferred payment*, the job money does when a debt or a
contract reaches across time, so that what is owed later can be reckoned
against what was agreed now.

To me, that fourth sits more naturally inside the second. The unit of account
splits in two, by *time*. There is a transitory unit — the yardstick you price
and quote in at the moment of exchange, needing only that both parties can
reason in it and reference it easily, which a wallet showing a live rate
already handles. And there is a contractual unit — the yardstick a ten-year
agreement is written in, where the hard problem is not convenience but holding
value steady over a long horizon. Jevons's standard of deferred payment is
really this contractual unit under another name. So I keep three functions and
split the second by time horizon, rather than carry a separate fourth. Hold that
split in view — it is where the later chapters do their real work.

### Which job comes first?

The schools of monetary thought disagree, and have long disagreed, about which
function is money's *essence*. The Mengerian, Austrian tradition puts the medium
of exchange first — money is, above all, the thing you spend. The chartalist,
Keynesian and credit tradition puts the unit of account first — money is, above
all, a way of *reckoning*, of keeping accounts and denominating obligations, with
the coin coming later. Nick Szabo and Saifedean Ammous insist it is store of value
first. That is an argument about *importance*. For the history, an older
question matters more: not which job is primary, but where money *came from*.

### Two stories about where money came from

The first story is the one most of us absorbed without noticing: money arose to
cure the awkwardness of barter — two people each needing what the other has, at
the same moment. It is the Mengerian barter origin, and it is so tidy it rings
true. The trouble is that the historical and anthropological record tells
against it on any significant scale. Call it the barter myth. The second story
is the one the evidence appears to primarily support.

### Born free

What the record actually shows is that money's jobs began *apart*. In
Mesopotamia, three thousand years before the common era, the silver shekel served
pervasively as the unit of account — the measure in which value was reckoned on
temple and palace ledgers — while payment itself moved in barley, in silver by
weight, or in netted credit. The thing that measured and the thing that changed
hands were routinely different things. Here David Graeber is a useful witness,
though a witness for a narrower point than his famous title suggests. His "five
thousand years" is a figure about *debt* and credit preceding coinage; it is not
a measure of any bind. Cited for its *evidence* — that virtual credit and units
of account came first — it testifies for the thesis of this booklet, showing the
functions originally dissociated. Cited for its *thesis* it would overreach us, so
it is the evidence we take, and only the evidence.

So the honest picture is an arc, not an event. In nine words: money's jobs were
born free, fused by coinage, then welded tight by modern fiat.

### Fused by coinage

Coinage is the fusion, and it happens in Lydia in the late 7th century BCE
(around 600 BCE) — the roughly 2,600-year anchor this booklet takes its subtitle
from. What a coin *does*, mechanically, is certify weight and purity *in advance*.
Once a stamped piece guarantees its own content, value can pass by count — by
tale — instead of by weighing and assaying at every exchange. That collapses the
cost of having one object do every job at once, and it is why the invention
spread and, tellingly, why it was invented more than once: China developed cast
bronze money and India almost certainly its punch-marked coins independently, in
the same few centuries, which is the mark of a genuine watershed rather than a
local accident.

The deeper mechanism beneath the fusion is *embodiment*. A coin physically
collapses medium, measure and store into a single locus — one small metal object
you can spend, reckon in, and hold. The functions bundle because they share a
*body*, not because any law of money requires it. That is the crucial point to
carry forward, and it is the whole "why now" of this booklet: for
twenty-six centuries, value has *had* a body.

### Aristotle, soon after

Coinage bedded in remarkably fast. By the time Aristotle was writing about money,
two or three centuries after the first coins, it was established enough that he
could treat its workings as familiar ground and describe them almost in passing.
He did not *list* the functions — the numbered triad with its familiar acronyms
is a much later tidying-up, and in any case the functions were not what most
interested him about money — but he described them clearly all the same: money as
a *measure* that makes unlike things commensurable (his own example weighs a
house against five beds), as an *intermediary* in exchange, and as a kind of
*surety* against dealings still to come. That so recognisable a picture could be
drawn so soon after the invention is itself the tell: in a couple of centuries a
stamped piece of metal had gone from novelty to the obvious way value worked.

### Fused, not welded

Coinage was not the only force pressing the functions together. Law is the other:
modern state money — legal tender, and the demand that taxes be paid in the
state's own unit — holds money's jobs in a single national instrument about as
tightly as they have ever been held. Yet even with the magnetic pull of coin and
law combined, the bind was never total. Wherever a single money began to fail at
one of its jobs, there is a long history of that job breaking free again.

Even after the coin was struck, then, the jobs kept pulling apart wherever it
paid to let them. Medieval Europe kept its books in "ghost" or imaginary money:
English accounts ran in pounds and shillings for centuries while, for long
stretches, only the penny was actually minted — the pound and shilling surviving
as pure units of account with no coin behind them. The French livre tournois did
the same work. These are not pre-coinage curiosities; they sit *after* the coin,
which is precisely the point. And it still happens: Chile's Unidad de Fomento,
introduced in 1967, is a purely contractual unit with no physical form, indexed
daily and used to denominate mortgages, loans, rents and pensions, while the peso
does the day-to-day spending — the unit of account breaking free once
more, exactly as before.

### Someone saw today coming

None of this is a surprise to monetary theory. Forty years ago a strand of work
now called the New Monetary Economics, or the BFH programme, argued that the
medium of exchange and the unit of account could in principle be separated —
Fischer Black in 1970, Eugene Fama in 1980, Robert Hall's commodity-basket
unit-of-account proposal, and Greenfield and Yeager in 1983. Yeager went so far
as to name the reflexive denial of the possibility the "unit-of-account
fallacy." The idea was not refuted. It was shelved — judged impractical for want
of the technology to carry it.

That is the hinge on which the rest turns. For 2,600 years the functions bundled
because value had to have a *body*, and a body can only be in one place doing one
job of moving, measuring and holding all at once. Programmable digital cash is
the first substrate since Lydia to lift the embodiment constraint. Once value is
information rather than an object, the functions no longer *have* to share a body
— and the theory that waited forty years for an engine finally has one.

## The multitool, and the fight it dissolves

### One handle, several tools

Picture a Swiss army knife. The blade, the corkscrew, the screwdriver, the little
scissors — each was a perfectly good tool on its own, and each existed on its own
long before anyone bound them into a single handle. What put them in one handle
was not some law of tools. It was circumstance: carrying one object that does six
jobs beat carrying six objects, given the cost of pockets, packs and the bother of
finding the right one.

Money is the same kind of object. Its tools are medium of exchange, the unit of
account and a store of value. They were bound into one handle for the reason
the last chapter traced — because, for twenty-six centuries, value had to have a
body, and one body carried more cheaply than several. Bundling tracked the *cost* 
of keeping things apart, not any deep
requirement that they be together. This is the same fact as before, seen from the
tool bench: the bundle is a transaction-cost artefact.

### The fight over which tool is the real one

Once you see money as a multitool, a lot of argument turns out to be a strange
quarrel: people insisting that *their* tool is the real one, and the rest mere
attachments. The most audible
version, in and around crypto, is a dispute over ordering. One school runs the
sequence hardness → store of value → medium of exchange: a money must first hold
value across time before anyone will accept it in trade, so store of value is
primary and everything else follows. Another puts the medium of exchange first —
money is what you can spend. A third, quieter voice, inherited from the
chartalist tradition, puts the unit of account first.

These are real positions held by serious people, and the point here is not to
knock any of them down. It is to notice their shape. Each takes the bundle as
*given* and then argues about which job inside it deserves top billing. Take the store-of-value-first ordering. It says hardness
must come first. But once the medium of exchange is cheap and programmable, store
of value can be supplied as an overlay a user picks — so a medium-first view, with
programmability, quietly *contains* the store-first view as a special case. That
is a reason to hold the ordering loosely, not a reason to declare a winner. All the contestants
are still arguing over the same handle.

### Walking out of the stadium

The unbundling thesis is not another entrant in that contest. It is orthogonal to
it. It is not a new team in the store-versus-medium league; it is a walk out of
the stadium altogether. If the tools can come apart — if you can carry the corkscrew
without the rest — then "which tool is the real one?" stops being a question
worth coming to blows over. You choose the tool the task in front of you needs.

This is exactly why the thesis is hard to hear from inside the sport. Everyone in
the stands is braced for a partisan — waiting to learn which money you are here to
crown — and this is not that. Refusing the match looks, at first, like refusing to
answer. It is instead answering a different, better question: not *which money
wins*, but *which function, for this use, right now*.

### One money to rule them all?

The case for money-maximalism — the belief that one money must eventually win
out and crowd the rest away — leans on a second idea from Menger: that money
*converges*, traders gravitating onto a single most-salable good until one money
crowds out its rivals. That is true, but it rests on a premise his world made
invisible: that switching between monies is expensive. Convergence is a
high-friction phenomenon. Digital cash drives that friction toward zero — and
when the premise relaxes, so does the conclusion. Menger was right about his
world; we have changed one of his constants.

### The honest limit

It would be easy to over-claim here, so I won't. Lowering friction *relaxes*
convergence pressure; it does not abolish it. A unit of account is a
common-knowledge good — it is useful precisely *because* other people also read it,
which is the definition of a network effect. Driving the mechanical cost of
switching to near-zero removes the law that once forced the functions to cohabit,
but it does not remove the coordinating gravity that keeps an established
yardstick in place. So the honest claim is modest and, for that reason, durable:
the unbundling bites hardest and first where the incumbent money is *already bad* —
long-horizon contracts under real currency-regime uncertainty, where no existing
option serves well — and not everywhere at once. The stickiness that looks like an
obstacle is exactly what makes a narrow wedge sufficient: you do not need the
whole economy to defect, only the sparsest, most exposed slice of it.

### It needn't — not it mustn't

Set beside that limit, the permissive character of the thesis matters more, not
less. The claim isn't that a single asset *must not* carry every function. It
is that it need not. If two parties freely re-bundle — choosing to transact, 
denominate and store the one asset because, for them, it is simplest — that is the
thesis operating exactly as advertised. The complaint is only ever against
bundling *compelled*: by friction that leaves no alternative, or by a state that
forbids one. Co-availability is the whole point. A freely chosen re-bundle is a
feature of a free menu, not a refutation of it.

### The one-line version

If the argument of this chapter had to be compressed to a single sentence it would
be this: only the medium of exchange need be intrinsic to the base asset; the unit
of account and the store of value can both be selectable overlay services, chosen
at the edge for the job at hand.

One honest caveat travels with that crisp line so it does not quietly promise more
than it can keep. "Intrinsic to the base asset" is a claim about *functional
selection* — about which jobs must be built into the coin itself and which can be
composed on top. It is not a claim about the underlying economics of security. The
overlay services still ride on a chain whose security is paid for by whoever
holds and uses the base coin. So what comes apart
is the *function*, cleanly and genuinely — while the plumbing beneath still leans on
the very demand the overlays let you re-express. Re-separating the jobs is not the
same as escaping what keeps the whole thing standing, and this booklet will not
pretend otherwise.

That is the tool laid out on the bench. The bind was a cost artefact; the cost is
vastly lowered; the tools can come apart. What remains — the substance of the
chapters ahead
— is the practical question of *how*: which function is delivered by what, at what
price, with which weaknesses named out loud.

## The functions already in hand

We've had the theory. Now the concrete.

Take money's three functions and lay them on Bitcoin Cash. Two are handled outright today — and so is half of the third. Medium of exchange, store of value, and the transitory half of the unit of account all have working answers, built from tools that already exist. What's left is the other half of the unit of account — the contractual one — and that is the open frontier. It gets its own chapter.

A note before I start. I'm writing this part for the home crowd. If you live in Bitcoin Cash you know AnyHedge, Paytaca and ParyonUSD cold, so I'll name them plainly and not stop to explain the basics. If you've wandered in from outside, the shape still holds — these are just some of the working parts that do each job.

### Medium of exchange — out of the box

Bitcoin Cash is a medium of exchange out of the box. You don't bolt anything on to spend it. Open a wallet, send some, it arrives. That much is intrinsic — it's just what the chain is.

For anyone ready for Bitcoin Cash to be their cash, carrying all four functions at once, that's fantastic. Prices can be agreed in it, it can be held as a store of value — a bouncy one — and the brave can even denominate long contracts in it. Nothing that follows takes any of that away.

But using Bitcoin Cash doesn't oblige us to use its base unit for all four functions, and that's the whole point of these two chapters. Of the four, only the medium of exchange really needs to be intrinsic to the money itself. The other three can be selectable services, chosen at the edge, per use. With solid base functionality as a medium of exchange, Bitcoin Cash's other technologies give us the option to let those functions operate independently.

So — medium of exchange: solved, and built in. Let me go through the rest.

### Transitory unit of account — the wallet already solved it

Second function: the unit of account. Earlier we split it in two, by time horizon.

The transitory unit is the one you reason in at the moment of exchange — the number on the price tag, the quote you're reading right now. Call it the transitory unit of account, the UoTA. The other is the one you write into a contract that runs for years — the unit of contractual account. Same function, split by time horizon, and very different jobs.

The transitory job is basically solved, and wallets solved it. Your wallet reads the live rate and shows you the price in whatever unit you think in. I can price in pounds, you can settle in BCH, and the wallet does the conversion in front of us both. We can even pick two different units — each of us sees our own — and the trade still clears. The unit floats free of the medium here, and nobody notices it happen.

There's one place it breaks: where your local pricing currency is itself unstable. In a hyperinflating economy even the transitory job is unsolved, because the thing you're reasoning in won't sit still for an afternoon. Hold that thought. It comes back in the fourth function.

### Store of value — rented, not embodied

Third function: store of value. What about people who don't want — or can't afford — their purchasing power to bounce the way Bitcoin Cash does? You don't have to have been in BCH long to have watched yours drop hard over a short stretch. That's not for everyone. Swapping in and out of fiat is an option, or stablecoins. But the most elegant answer for me — keeping the store-of-value function apart from the base unit while staying inside the Bitcoin Cash ecosystem — is AnyHedge.

Once holding is separable from the medium, store of value stops being a property baked into the coin and becomes a service you rent from a market. That is what AnyHedge is. You take some BCH, fix its value against a reference you choose — say US dollars or gold — for a term. The number of coins in the contract that are yours moves up and down; the value, measured in the asset you chose, stays constant. You're passing the fluctuation risk you don't want to a contract partner who does. At the end of the term the BCH is freed back into plain medium of exchange, for the next owner to re-select whatever they want.

Two things fall out of this that are stronger than "just pick a stablecoin".

First, each party picks the numeraire for their own side. It isn't one shared choice of store of value for the whole deal. I can fix my side against dollars while you leave yours in BCH, or fix yours against something else entirely. The function unbundles across the two parties, not just across assets.

Second — and this matters — it needs no token. There's no coin to hold, no secondary market, no peg to defend. It's just a contract between two parties referencing a value. There are caveats, but Paytaca has a layer that takes care of them: one tap and your balance holds steady against the US dollar.

So: three functions, three answers, all on the one base layer, each picked per use. Use Bitcoin Cash for all of them if that suits you — or reach for the wallet's live rate for the transitory unit, and a rentable hedge for the store of value. None of it is hidden inside a single price. It's a menu, and you choose from it.

That leaves the other half — the contractual unit.

## The remaining 'half-function' — a unit of contractual account

Two functions and a half down, and each had a working answer. The remaining half doesn't — or didn't, until a feed quietly went live in June. It's the one genuinely open frontier, and the solving the big-picture problem is a hard one.

This is the unit of contractual account, the UoCA: the yardstick you write into a deal that runs for years — a mortgage, a long lease, a pension, a long-dated cross-border supply agreement. Choosing it is the same balancing of trade-offs as the other functions, only harder. A store of value is allowed to shoot up; nobody minds an SoV that gains. But a unit two parties will settle against in ten years' time needs to stay within a range of real-world value that won't, at maturity, hand one of them a windfall at the other's expense.

### A unit that already exists

The frontier isn't science fiction. A pure unit of contractual account already runs, at national scale, and has for over half a century. Chile has the Unidad de Fomento. It has no physical form — no coin, no note, nothing to hold or bank or spend. It's a number, recomputed daily and indexed to inflation, and Chileans denominate mortgages, loans, rents and pensions in it while paying in pesos. That's the split, live: the UF does the long-horizon reckoning, the peso the actual paying.

Most long contracts elsewhere do something similar in spirit — priced in fiat, then index-linked to CPI. But look at what that convenience rests on. You need confidence that whoever picks the basket picks the right items, at the right weightings, for both parties. You need an authority, and you both need to trust it. And you need to trust it will stay honest for the length of the deal — including through the black swan, the currency collapse, the hyperinflation, which is exactly when an authority is most tempted to massage the number — or, if things are bad enough, to stop publishing it altogether. National indices also travel badly across borders. The UF is a necessary instrument — an elegant patch on a problem born of chronic inflation — but every one of those trust points is still load-bearing inside it.

### The mechanism

Start with what an oracle normally does. An oracle is the bridge a smart contract uses to reach a fact it cannot see for itself: it reaches out into the world, fetches a price, and transmits it onto the chain. Value flows from the outside in.

We've just seen the trouble with today's contractual units — the basket, the authority, the trust that it stays honest. bitcoincashautist's work brings something neat to light: the answer to our contractual-unit question is already sitting there, in the inner workings of Bitcoin Cash mining. We don't have to invent a number — we can just read one that already exists, and feed it the other way, from inside the chain out into the world, as a verifiable figure any two contracting parties can lean on. Because it's drawn from the chain's own workings, it inherits the security of the blockchain itself. Feeding out like this — the opposite way round from a normal oracle — is why I call it a reverse oracle.

The switch in perspective bitcoincashautist invites us to make runs like this. The network is buying a commodity — hashes — and miners spend real electricity to make them. The difficulty adjustment is what clears that market: an automated market-maker for hashpower, running with no external feed at all. Every block is a cleared trade, so every block is a price, readable from the block header alone. That difficulty carries BCH's own price inside it — but divide the work by the miners' total revenue — the block reward plus fees — and BCH's own price cancels out, leaving Bitcoin Cash measured against the global price of energy. (The detail, algebra and limitations are in bitcoincashautist's published works.)

And because the number is computed from consensus data — what the whole network already agrees on, produced by no one on purpose — no amount of token trading can move it. The same mining security that protects Bitcoin Cash from double-spends protects this unit from being shoved around. And here's the thing that has blown my mind: the feed is the product; a token, if there is one, is just a wrapper.

### Why energy

A word for it: steadycoin. A stablecoin promises a peg and can break it; a steadycoin promises a trend — a long average, honest that it will drift.

Why anchor to energy at all? Because there's no such thing as absolute stability. Every numeraire holds one thing fixed and floats against everything else; the only question is what you fix, and how corruptible it is.

| Instrument | Anchored to | Kind |
|---|---|---|
| The US dollar | US monetary policy | political |
| A US-dollar stablecoin | the dollar, via an issuer | political + custodial (mostly) |
| Chile's UF | the official CPI | statistical + political |
| Volatile crypto | supply and demand | market |
| A steadycoin | energy | physical |

A steadycoin swaps a political or statistical anchor for a physical one, set by no one. And energy is close to a master economic variable — panned out far enough, there's a strong historical connection between per-capita energy use and both GDP and quality-of-life measures — so it's a more reasonable thing to reckon long-horizon value against than it first sounds.

Which reframes the obvious question. You'll want to ask: is it accurate? But there's no honest inflation index out there to be accurate against — CPIs are gamed, revised and distrusted. The real bar isn't precision; it's whether the number is unbiased and its reference too expensive to bend. This one costs real electricity to move, not the price of lying in a data feed.

So, yes, a trade-off. It's not an accurate proxy for the price of electricity — miners are speculators too, and it has real peaks and troughs. But for the cost of inaccuracy, your gain is freedom from any authority choosing and pricing a basket, and of the need to trust they'll keep publishing it honestly. And in a currency collapse, as long as an energy market and sufficient connectivity for proof-of-work mining to survive, the unit stays tethered to the real world in a way possibly nothing else can. If you want the full account of its limits, they're set out in bitcoincashautist's work — please read it before relying on any of this.

### Why not bitcoin, or gold?

The old argument says bitcoin, with its fixed supply, is the ideal inflation hedge. Perhaps a usable-as-money bitcoin becomes that one day. But in the meantime there's huge fluctuation risk; no guarantee which crypto wins; and, worst for a contract, the risk of a one-way, irreversible move — to the floor or to the moon. That's too big a one-sided risk for a unit two parties must settle against fairly.

Gold is the other reflex. But gold still has to be *priced* — and its usable contract price is a fiat quote from an institution, which in a real crisis fragments across jurisdictions with capital controls and export bans, dragging the trusted party and the fiat back in exactly when you needed neither. The energy unit is self-denominating: its reference is quoted in nothing and produced by nobody. On the shelf of trust-minimised references — volatile crypto, stablecoins that inherit their peg's fate, gold, corruptible government indices — its energy-drift is the mildest defect on offer.

And that drift is symmetric: neither party knows which way it will go, and neither can rig it. The pitch to a counterparty is simply — neither of us can rig this, and neither of us knows who it'll favour. The unpredictability is the credibility.

### Who it's for

That tells you the market. Against the adversary of a collapsing currency, a rough energy proxy is a rounding error. So it suits parties on opposite sides of the world, contracting over long horizons, who share no currency they both trust and can't count on any government's index staying honest for the term. It needs no network effect — just two parties agreeing it's the best option for their purpose. And it isn't only for crypto-native deals: indexed contracts are generally enforceable given a clearly specified formula, with historical gold-clause contracts — denominated in a real measure, settled in any currency — as the direct precedent.

### A neat test of neutrality

I intend to be among the first to use this, denominating a real contract of my own in it. Normally a publisher of the unit sitting on one side of the deal would be a glaring conflict of interest. Here it isn't — because I can't bend the number, whichever side I'm on. If it's fair when the unit's publisher is your counterparty and still can't move it, it's fair.

To me a significant historic event quietly coming into being buried in the cashnode.bch.ninja charts (hosted by Kallisti), with the code public at gitlab.com/0353F40E/aggi. This isn't theory. It's here — at least in its essential form.

I will be publishing the number at *reverseoracle.cash*, with a link back to the source, and alongside it a long-tail weighted average (weighting and duration still to be decided) that's less swayed by any single day or month, and so may suit longer contracts better. A number on a website is just a number on a website — but the power of this one is that, while my site could be unreliable, the hash-market data and its published formula can be verified by anyone, so both parties can check it independently at maturity.

Turning that feed into a trust-minimised oracle consumable by Bitcoin Cash smart contracts is the next step. bitcoincashautist frames this as a *native oracle* because it doesn't reach outside the chain — it's *internal*. 

The token side is also moving fast. bitcoincashautist's proposed steadycoin may be coming soon as a Paryon fork, and — announced just this week — a token based on similar principles (a minable cash token) has been released by 2qx. This is a lively corner right now, so get interested and get involved. You'll find both on Bitcoin Cash Research: for bitcoincashautist's native-oracle and steadycoin work [https://bitcoincashresearch.org/t/research-block-difficulty-as-a-price-oracle] with 2qx's token thread on [https://bitcoincashresearch.org/t/block-tops-btop-a-minable-cashtoken].

One caution once tokens enter the picture. The market that sets today's published feed and tomorrow's oracle output is isolated, and isn't directly arbitraged. That means in thin conditions a secondary-market token can drift from the true unit. A contract using this as its unit of contractual account should therefore specify that it's the oracle-published number that counts — never any token's market price.

### You don't even need a token

It's worth seeing the levels this comes in. At the first, the unit is just a calculation — a yardstick, needing only the 'invisible', isolated hash market to be correct. At the second, two parties reference it through an AnyHedge contract, one renting stability from the other — still no token, and for me this is the natural first home for the feed, since AnyHedge payouts are already oracle-determined. Only at the third does a token need to appear, for those who want something bearer and composable. The native feed is one instrument, a long-horizon smoothed unit (my proposal above) is another; they do different jobs and may well have different builders. The point worth holding is that the breakthrough is the feed itself — the token is optional.

### The close

That's the contractual unit — the last of money's jobs to find an answer. Not solved — but for the first time it has a credible shape: a unit computed by no one, quoted in nothing, that neither party to a contract can bend and neither can predict. *And* the fledgeling version of which is live today.

Should it becomes tokenised, it can serve as a medium of exchange too; and if people start pricing in it, we may end up with all of money's functions resting on this one unit after all. If so, so be it — that was always the point. I've never claimed that one thing holding all of money's functions is wrong. The claim is only that now, on Bitcoin Cash, we have every one of them available in some form — and so we have the choice: to bundle or not, as best suits us and the people we trade with.

#### The history and the theory

- W. S. Jevons, *Money and the Mechanism of Exchange* (1875) — the four
  functions, and the fourth that most people forget.
- Carl Menger, *On the Origin of Money* (1892) — both the barter-origin story I
  set aside and the convergence argument I keep.
- David Graeber, *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (2011) — cited for its evidence
  that credit and units of account came before coinage, not for its wider thesis.
- Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* (Book V) and *Politics* (Book I) — money as
  measure, intermediary, and surety.
- The unbundling was theorised decades ago as the New Monetary Economics, or the
  "BFH" proposals — Fischer Black, Eugene Fama, Robert Hall, and Greenfield &
  Yeager, across the 1970s and early '80s.
- Chile's Unidad de Fomento (from 1967) — a living unit of contractual account.
- On medieval "ghost money", Luigi Einaudi's essay on imaginary money is the
  classic way in.
- Nick Szabo — Shelling Out: The Origins of Money (2002) https://nakamotoinstitute.org/library/shelling-out 
- Saifedean Ammous — The Bitcoin Standard (2018).

the place to look.
